Project 001: ED-to-Inpatient Pathways

From Emergency Department to Inpatient Care: Admission, Length of Stay and Asthma-Related Risk in Simulated Alberta Administrative Data

Project 001 follows patients along one care pathway using a single shared synthetic dataset of Alberta-style NACRS and DAD records. It asks which factors are linked to being admitted and to how long patients then stay, and goes on to study admission risk and regional differences for adults who come to the emergency department with asthma.

It tackles two kinds of problem at once: health services research questions, and the methods question of how to answer them credibly and reproducibly. Memos A00–A09 connect data preparation, record linkage, statistical analysis and simulation checks into one complete workflow, and A09 summarizes the whole project.

One patient pathway, two data sources

  1. Emergency visitDiagnosis, triage acuity, timing and hospital
  2. Admitted or notThe disposition at the end of the ED visit
  3. Length of stayHow long the inpatient stay lasts
NACRS
One record per emergency visit: main diagnosis, triage level (CTAS), visit times, hospital and whether the patient was admitted. 30,000 ED visits, 50 analytic variables.
DAD
One record per inpatient stay: admission, discharge and length of stay. 60,000 inpatient records, 64 analytic variables.

Each of the 30,000 ED visits is a different patient. The 5,810 admitted visits each continue as one DAD abstract at the same hospital, and the other 54,190 DAD abstracts are inpatients from outside the ED extract, so the 90,000 rows are not 90,000 independent patients. Every memo draws on these two sources.

The first two questions are the project's original focus. The link between ED time and admission cannot be read as "the longer you wait, the more likely you are to be admitted": how complex the case is, the tests it needs and bed availability all shape that time. Waiting after the decision to admit, in particular, cannot be a cause of a decision that came before it. A03's main analysis therefore uses ED time up to the disposition decision, and both A03 and A05 are associational analyses.

What the original questions found

Admission (A03)

Triage level dominates. Compared with CTAS 1, the adjusted odds of admission fall at every level, to an odds ratio of 0.056 at CTAS 5. Time before the disposition decision has only a small adjusted association: OR 1.024 per 60 minutes (95% CI 1.003 to 1.045).

Linkage (A04)

4,633 of 5,810 admitted visits (79.7%) link to exactly one DAD abstract under the rule "same PHN, admitted within 6 hours of ED discharge". Every unlinked visit traces to a defect built into the simulation: 888 missing or mistyped identifiers and 289 late admissions.

Length of stay (A05)

On its own, a longer ED stay looks linked to a longer inpatient stay (+0.147 days per 60 minutes), but the association disappears once CTAS is included: −0.011 days per 60 minutes (95% CI −0.056 to 0.034). Stays after a CTAS 5 visit are 2.78 days shorter than after CTAS 1.

Linked inpatient stays average 4.79 days (median 4). Acute stay, which excludes alternate-level-of-care days, gives the same picture. These are associations in simulated data, not causal effects.

Adult asthma: building a fairer comparison

The asthma study moves the project from describing associations to designing a fair comparison. From NACRS it selects visits by patients aged 18 or over whose main diagnosis for that visit is in the asthma J45 family. The study population is people who came to the ED because of asthma this time, not everyone with a history of asthma and not everyone presenting with shortness of breath. The shared data hold 24,926 adult ED visits, of which 8,170 have asthma as the main diagnosis.

A straight comparison of admission rates would be unfair, because the asthma group may differ in age, smoking, obesity, medical history and past ED use. A06 therefore uses propensity scores to find non-asthma patients with similar backgrounds at the same hospital, producing 7,700 matched pairs. After matching, admission is 31.8% in the asthma group and 21.8% in the controls: a risk difference of 10.00 points (95% CI 7.59 to 12.41). A07 keeps those same pairs and standardizes predicted risks with a logistic outcome model.

These estimates apply to the asthma visits that were successfully matched. Matching improves comparability on measured background factors, but it does not remove all confounding, and matching alone cannot prove a causal effect in the real world.

Two regional questions

Among asthma patients only

When age, medical history and severity on arrival are comparable, does the probability of admission differ across the three regions?

Asthma versus other visits

Does the extra admission risk associated with asthma differ by region? This is the asthma × region interaction in the model. The two questions are related but mean different things.

The 30 hospitals are split evenly across three study regions. Calgary and Edmonton include hospitals in surrounding towns, and Other is made up of selected hospitals from the Central, North and South zones. Patients are grouped by the region of the hospital they attended, not where they live. A08 fits this on all 24,926 adult visits, not the matched pairs: a logistic GLMM with region as a fixed effect and a random intercept for hospital. In the simulation, adult asthma patients in Edmonton have a higher standardized admission probability than those in other regions (+7.23 points, 95% CI 2.16 to 12.99). Calgary's estimate is lower (−5.72 points, 95% CI −10.53 to 0.25), but that interval includes zero, so the direction is not established. The gap between asthma and other visits does differ clearly by region (joint test χ² = 78.0 on 2 degrees of freedom, p < 0.001). A higher or lower admission rate is not, on its own, a measure of better or worse care.

Can the methods recover the truth?

Because the patient data are simulated, the true effects built into the data are known. The project can therefore generate many datasets with and without an effect and check whether matching, regression and mixed models are biased and whether their confidence intervals can be trusted. That says far more about how a method performs than running one model once and reading off a p-value.

Where the project stands

The shared data, memos A00–A09, the main models and the simulation checks are all complete. A09 is the project summary: it restates the design and collects the results from A00 through A08. What the project offers is a reproducible worked example of ED-to-inpatient research, along with a test of the methods used. Whether these effects exist among real Alberta patients is a question only research on real data can answer.

The names of the 30 hospitals come from real public sources, but the patients, admission outcomes and hospital and regional effects are all simulated. None of these results can be used to judge any real hospital.

Project 002: Calgary Housing Observatory

Calgary Housing by Community: Prices, Structure Mix and Spatial Patterns in a Residential Listings Extract

An interactive R Shiny dashboard on 21,328 Calgary residential listings, mapped onto the City of Calgary's official community boundaries. Filter by price, year built, sector, structure type and bedrooms; the community map, charts and community table update together.

The public version is community level by design: no owner names, addresses or MLS numbers, and no listing-level table.
